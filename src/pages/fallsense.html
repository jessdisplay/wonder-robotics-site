Most of our work starts with somebody else's building. This one started on a bench here: a shallow disc that mounts on the ceiling of a care home bedroom and works out what is happening below it without ever making a picture.

Florence is its own company with its own name, palette and type, built here alongside the device. Fall Sense is the first thing it sells. There is no arm in it and nothing moves.

The problem

A resident goes down at two in the morning. The call button is on the wall by the bed and they are on the floor by the bathroom, so it may as well be in another building. Nobody finds out until the round.

The obvious fix is a camera in the bedroom, and every operator who has tried it has run into the same wall. Residents will not have it, families will not have it, and they are right. A bedroom is somebody's home.

What it does about it

It mounts flush on the ceiling, one to a room, and it works out what is happening below it without ever producing a picture a person could look at. What leaves the unit is the event: somebody is on the floor, somebody has been in the bathroom for forty minutes, somebody has not got out of bed this morning.

That is the whole design argument. There is no footage to leak, no screen for a night staffer to sit in front of, and nothing for a family to object to on a tour.

The stack

A round unit that recesses into the ceiling on two spring clips, the way a good in-ceiling speaker does, with a fine woven mesh face and a thin ring of warm light at the rim that is the only thing it ever shows. No visible screws, no vents, no lens you can see. It is designed to the standard of the objects people already choose to have in their homes, because a care home bedroom is one. Edge compute on the board, so the model runs in the room. Python and Postgres behind it, and the same dashboard our kitchen managers use, because we had already written it.

Firmware updates over the air. When one stops reporting, it tells us before it tells you.
