The brief

Little Red Dumpling has thirteen shops open across Queensland and one menu across all of them. The fourteenth, at Nexus Way in the Gold Coast Health and Knowledge Precinct, was to cook that menu without a brigade behind the pass, in a long narrow tenancy with a raked roof four metres up and glazing the length of one wall.

The ask was the whole job: the kitchen, the front of house, the ordering, the screens, and the people who answer the phone after it opens.

Planning

A fixed menu across thirteen venues is the best case a robot kitchen gets. The arm learns one set of moves and repeats them all day, so the payback is on wages at the pass, not on novelty. We scoped it against the real tenancy on the Gold Coast, an hour down the M1 from our own kitchen, which is where the client came to watch the same line run before committing.

What the machines cost, what they save on a night shift, and what the whole fitout came to are in the quote, and go on this page when the client is happy for them to.

Design

The tenancy runs long and narrow with the glazing down one side, so the cook line went on the blind wall and the seating went against the glass. The plan sets out the rail the arm travels on, the six wells, the pass and the counter. The section is the one that decided the job: four metres of roof meant the extraction and the rail could both hang, and the arm could work above the counter instead of beside it.

The brand pass is the board. Seven dishes, three drinks lines, the photography shot dish by dish because a bowl has to survive being seen on a screen at knee height in a busy room. It runs on the CadeBot and on the order screens, and at Nexus Way it carries the Nexus menu.

Supply

A six-axis arm on a linear rail down the cook line, working behind glass so the room can watch it. Six boiling wells for dumplings and noodles, bamboo steamers stacked at the pass, extraction the length of the counter. A UBTECH CadeBot for the floor, forty kilos of bowls a trip. Everything under the counter is ours too: the ordering, the payment, the stock counts, the dashboard the manager watches on a phone. The same stack that runs our own kitchen in Fortitude Valley, set to a single-menu, high-volume dumpling operation.

Implementation

The shell came to us as studs and a slab. First in was the stainless bench frame down the blind wall, then the linear rail above it, because everything else is set out from the rail. The extraction canopy hung off the trusses, the conduit and cable tray ran, and the arm sat crated on the slab until the bench was level to a millimetre, which is the tolerance the rail wants.

The week before opening is the week nobody photographs. Tables against the wall, the glass open, the arm running the plating cycle over empty bowls two hundred times while somebody watches the wrist and somebody else watches the wells. Every recipe on the menu gets timed on this line before a customer sees it, and the order screens, the payment and the stock counts are all live against real bowls by the end of it.

The CadeBot runs the aisle between the pass and the tables along the glazing. It talks to the same ordering system the arm does, so a bowl leaves the pass with a table number already on it, and the staff are trained on it the same week the arm is.

Maintenance

The arm and the wells report to us. A service visit on a schedule, spares on the shelf in the Valley an hour up the motorway, software updates overnight when the line is cold, and a Brisbane number that answers when it is not. Priced on the same quote as the machines, so the client knew the running cost before the first bowl.

The outcome

Not open yet. The machines are delivered and the fitout is under way. The opening date, the bowls an hour the line actually does, and the photography go here the week it opens.
